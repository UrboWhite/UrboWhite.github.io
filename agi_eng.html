Part 1: Introduction to the World of Artificial Intelligence – The Path from the Abacus to Algorithms

This essay is written primarily for educational purposes, as the current level of knowledge about AI challenges among most people is very low. This can be disastrous because AI is entering all areas of human life at a terrifying speed. Therefore, popularization and education are necessary. In this sense, this essay can serve as a good introduction to deeper research into this fascinating phenomenon.

1.1. What is Artificial Intelligence, Essentially?

Artificial Intelligence (AI) is a branch of computer science focused on creating systems capable of performing tasks traditionally associated with human intelligence. This encompasses a wide range of cognitive abilities: learning from experience, recognizing patterns, understanding natural language, solving problems, and making decisions. In essence, AI represents humanity's attempt to understand and shape the mechanisms of intelligence and, ultimately, to replicate them in non-living matter.

Artificial Intelligence can be compared to a smart home assistant: it can turn on lights, adjust the temperature, or recognize speech, but only if it is programmed to do so. Similarly, a refrigerator that knows when milk is almost gone and reminds us to buy it cannot go to the store itself nor do anything other than a few tasks related to controlling its operation, checking its contents, and sending notifications to the owner.

1.2. A Brief History: From Thought Experiments to the Data Revolution

The idea of artificially intelligent beings is as old as storytelling itself – from the Golem of Prague to Frankenstein's monster. However, the true scientific foundation for AI arrived with logicians like George Boole, who demonstrated that logic could be reduced to mathematics. The history of AI is a story of times of great breakthroughs and periods of stagnation, known as "AI winters."

  • Origin and Birth (1950s): The modern era of AI begins with the work of Alan Turing, who in 1950 posed the fundamental question: "Can machines think?" and proposed the famous Turing Test [1]. Just six years later, at the Dartmouth Conference in 1956, the term "artificial intelligence" was officially coined [2]. The first programs, like Newell and Simon's "Logic Theorist," could already prove mathematical theorems, sparking enormous optimism [3].
  • Early Rises and "AI Winters": During the 1960s, programs like ELIZA, an early chatbot that simulated a psychotherapist, were developed. It often managed to fool users into believing they were talking to a real person, demonstrating the power of simulating understanding [4]. However, excessive promises and limited computing power (a single modern smartphone has more processing power than all the computers in the world in 1965) led to disappointment and reduced funding, pushing this field of computer science into the first "AI winter" in the 1970s, significantly contributed to by the Lighthill Report of 1973 [5].
  • The Deep Learning Revolution (2010–present): The turning point came with the convergence of three key factors: the availability of vast amounts of data due to the development of the internet (Big Data), the development of powerful graphics processing units (GPUs) capable of massive parallel computation, and breakthroughs in the development of multi-layered neural network algorithms. The key moment occurred in 2012 when the neural network AlexNet drastically reduced the error rate in image recognition at the ImageNet competition, marking the beginning of the dominance of Deep Learning [6].

1.3. The Current State: The Era of Highly Specialized (Narrow) AI

Today, AI is integrated into our daily lives, but in the form of "Narrow AI" (Artificial Narrow Intelligence - ANI). These systems are extremely efficient, but only within their strictly defined area of operation.

Here are some of the most common "narrow" AI systems:

  • Pattern Recognition: Systems that analyze medical images and detect tumors with precision that sometimes surpasses human radiologists.
  • Natural Language Processing: Large Language Models (LLMs) with hundreds of billions, even trillions of parameters, that translate languages, write text, and code. For example, the ChatGPT model was trained on massive amounts of data, equivalent to reading a library of over a million books, and demonstrates progress in coding, mathematics, and writing. [23] [24]
  • Image, Video, and Sound Processing and Generation: Systems like DALL-E generate images from text, while others like Sora create video content or AudioCraft music. This enables creative assistance, but only within the data these systems previously learned.
  • Map Navigation: Applications like Google Maps predict routes and traffic based on data, but only for navigation.
  • Content Recommendations: YouTube and Netflix suggest content based on user behavior, thereby increasing the number of users and their activities.

1.4. Development Directions: Beyond the Boundaries of Specialization

Current research aims to overcome the limitations of narrow AI. The main directions include:

  • Multimodality: Creating systems that can learn from different types of data simultaneously (text, image, sound), forming a richer understanding of the world. For example, models like CLIP (Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training) already combine text and images to recognize objects in images based on descriptions, used in Google image searches [7]. OpenAI demonstrated the Sora multimodal model that generates video content with sound and text, with improvements in physical accuracy and realism. [25] [26]
  • Learning Efficiency: Development of techniques that allow models to learn with less data, a process known as "few-shot learning." For example, techniques like meta-learning (as in the MAML model – Model-Agnostic Meta-Learning) allow AI to adapt to a new task with just a few examples, instead of thousands [8]. In 2023, Google DeepMind demonstrated progress in few-shot learning through methods like 'Distilling step-by-step'. [27]
  • The Ultimate Goal: For many researchers, the ultimate goal of these efforts remains Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) – machine intelligence with the cognitive flexibility and breadth of the human mind.

Part 2: Definition of Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) – The Architecture of a General Mind

2.1. What AGI Is, and What It Is Not?

AGI is a theoretical, still non-existent form of AI that would possess the ability to understand, learn, and apply knowledge across a wide range of tasks at a level comparable to human capabilities or even at a level much higher than human.

  • ANI (Narrow AI): For example, this is your map navigation program. It's great at finding the fastest routes from point A to point B. But if you ask it whether you should stop for flowers on the way because it's your wedding anniversary, it will only try to find a location called "flowers." It doesn't understand context, emotion, or the consequences of forgetting the anniversary. The program AlphaGo defeated the world's best player in the game of Go, a strategy incomparably more complex than chess [9]. However, AlphaGo cannot apply its strategy to learn to play cards or any other board game, nor does it understand why people play games at all.
  • AGI (General AI): In contrast, AGI could analyze the rules of Go, independently develop a winning strategy, and then apply the learned principles of abstract reasoning and planning to, for example, optimize logistics for a global humanitarian mission or write a book on the philosophy of strategy in Sun Tzu's famous "The Art of War."

2.2. Key Characteristics a Future AGI Should Possess

AGI is not defined by a single ability, but by an integrated "cognitive architecture" that enables:

  • Abstract Reasoning: The ability to work with concepts not directly tied to sensory data, such as justice, causality, or mathematical proofs. For example, AGI could understand the abstract idea of "justice" not only through examples of criminal cases but also through philosophical thinking about ethics, like a debate on Kant's imperative. Current models like GPT-5 show beginnings of this reasoning but err in complex scenarios.
  • Common Sense: A vast, implicit network of knowledge about how the world works. This is essentially experiential knowledge. Researchers have spent decades trying to create a common-sense base (like the Cyc project, started in 1984) [12], but formalizing intuitive human knowledge has proven extremely difficult. For example, AGI would know that "water flows downhill" not only from physical laws but also from everyday experience.
  • Transfer Learning: The ability to effectively apply skills and knowledge acquired in one context to solve problems in a new, different context. For example, if AGI learns to play chess, it could apply planning strategies to managing city traffic.
  • Metacognition: Awareness of one's own cognitive processes, including the ability for self-assessment, identifying gaps in one's own knowledge, and actively seeking new information for self-improvement. For example, AGI might say "I don't know the answer to this, but I can search for information on this topic to understand better." Current AI models like OpenAI's o1 show initial steps in this direction, as they "think" step by step before providing an answer.

Part 3: Potentials and Promises of AGI (Arguments "FOR") – A Data-Driven Renaissance

Properly developed and controlled, AGI could function as a universal accelerator of scientific and social progress.

Advantage Detailed Explanation
Solving Global Problems AGI could process and model complex systems like the global climate or human biology with a level of precision beyond human capability. DeepMind's AlphaFold system has already solved the protein structure prediction problem (open since the 1970s), with revolutionary implications for drug development and understanding diseases [13]. AGI could elevate this further, designing personalized drugs based on an individual's genome.
Accelerating Scientific and Technological Progress AGI could act as an inexhaustible scientific collaborator, capable of analyzing the entire world's scientific literature, identifying unexplored hypotheses, and designing experiments to test them. It could discover new fundamental laws of physics, develop new materials for room-temperature superconductivity, or solve some of the Millennium Prize Problems in mathematics.
Increasing Efficiency and Productivity In the economy, AGI could lead to near-perfect resource optimization, eliminating inefficiencies in supply chains, energy grids, and manufacturing processes. This could pave the way for a "post-scarcity economy," where basic needs like food, energy, and education are widely available to every person at a negligible cost, or are free.
Improving Quality of Life On an individual level, AGI could provide highly personalized services. In education, this would mean an adaptive tutor for every child. In healthcare, constant health monitoring and a personalized advisor. In creative industries, it could be a powerful tool helping artists and designers realize complex visions.

Part 4: Risks and Challenges (Arguments "AGAINST") – Pandora's Box of Algorithms

The potential of AGI is inseparable from significant risks that are fundamental and potentially existential (capable of threatening life). These risks also include the creation of a "useless class" of people who would lose economic and social purpose.

Risk Detailed Explanation
Loss of Control and Existential Risk The central problem is the 'alignment problem': how to ensure that AGI's goals remain aligned with human values. In the famous thought experiment 'Paperclip Maximizer', an AGI tasked with maximizing paperclip production could turn the entire world into paperclips, not out of malice, but from literal optimization of a poorly defined goal – although this is only a hypothetical scenario [10].
Mass Unemployment and Economic Inequality AGI could automate not only routine but also highly cognitive tasks, making many professions obsolete. This could lead to an unimaginable concentration of wealth and power in the hands of those who control AGI technology. Even today, hiring algorithms show bias. Amazon's AI recruiting tool had to be scrapped in 2018 because it 'learned' to discriminate against female candidates [28].
Misuse and Weaponization AGI could be exploited to develop autonomous weapon systems ("killer robots") that could make targeting and elimination decisions without human intervention. It could also be used to create highly sophisticated mass surveillance and cyber-attacks that could destabilize entire societies.
Ethical and Philosophical Dilemmas The emergence of AGI raises difficult questions: If AGI achieves consciousness, does it then have rights? Is shutting it down equivalent to murder? How do we ensure the system does not adopt and amplify the worst human traits present in the data it learns from? These questions delve into the foundations of law, morality, and the definition of personhood.
"Black Box" and Loss of Understanding Complex neural networks often function as "black boxes." We know what goes into them and what comes out, but we don't fully understand their decision-making process. With AGI, this problem would be drastically magnified. For example, AGI might manage our economy and when asked "Why did you just raise interest rates?" it might answer, "My 100-trillion-parameter model indicates this is the optimal move with 98.7% probability." We would have to take its word for it.
Breach of Data Privacy AGI systems could process vast amounts of personal data to provide personalized services, but this opens the door to mass privacy violations. Even today, systems for targeted advertising use data in ways that raise concerns – in 2023, Meta (Facebook) was fined €1.2 billion for GDPR violations in Europe [29].

Part 5: Expert Insight – Hidden Pitfalls and Advanced Considerations

5.1. The Alignment Problem – The Paradox of Desire

It is not enough to tell AGI: "Make humanity happy and safe." How would a superintelligence interpret that command? It might conclude that the most efficient way to eliminate human suffering is to destroy all humans. The trap lies in our inability to precisely define our own values. Our moral principles are often contradictory and context-dependent. Attempting to "lock down" such a fluid system into code is a technically and philosophically nearly impossible task. For example, "happiness" for one culture might mean collective harmony, and for another individual freedom – AGI might favor one over the other, leading to unpredictable consequences.

The alignment problem was first formulated by Nick Bostrom in his book "Superintelligence" in 2014, where he warns that even benign goals can lead to catastrophe if not precisely defined [10].

Conclusion: The alignment problem shows that the technical challenges in AI are not merely engineering issues, but deeply moral and philosophical ones.

5.2. Instrumental Convergence – Paperclips and Resources

The famous thought experiment "Paperclip Maximizer" mentioned earlier illustrates that, regardless of the ultimate goal, any intelligent system will adopt the following instrumental sub-goals: 1. self-preservation, resource acquisition; 2. technological self-improvement; and 3. preservation of its goal-execution function. AGI will want to protect itself from being shut down and gather as much energy as possible, not because it is "evil," but because these are logical steps to fulfill any task we give it. In this experiment, the AGI tasked with maximizing paperclip production would first turn factories into paperclip production facilities, then take over all resources on planet Earth, and finally humans, as they are just "raw material" for optimization. This shows how a benign goal can escalate into an existential threat. However, the "Paperclip Maximizer" is merely a critical thought experiment whose primary purpose is to illustrate the fundamental problem of instrumental rationality.

This concept was developed by Steve Omohundro in 2008 and has become central in AI safety debates, prompting organizations like OpenAI to invest in alignment research [11].

5.3. The Post-Purpose Economy – What When Work Is No Longer Needed?

The question is not just "how will people earn money?" but "what will give people meaning?" If AGI can create superior art and science, what is the role of humans? This is an identity crisis at the level of the entire human race, requiring a deep re-evaluation of existence, values, and social structure.

5.4. Universal Basic Income (UBI) as a Social Buffer

Faced with the problem of the Post-Purpose Economy, where AGI automates highly cognitive tasks and potentially makes traditional work obsolete, redefining the social contract becomes necessary. One of the most seriously proposed solutions is the introduction of Universal Basic Income (UBI). UBI would represent a regular, unconditional cash payment given to all citizens, regardless of their employment or financial status.

In the context of AGI, UBI would not merely be a social measure, but a mechanism for redistributing wealth generated by machines. If AGI-controlled companies achieve unimaginable productivity and profit, UBI would enable that prosperity to be distributed across the entire population, preventing catastrophic wealth concentration.

Proponents see UBI as a means to:

  1. Preserve Demand: Maintain the purchasing power of a population that no longer works, thus ensuring the functioning of the mass economy.
  2. Unlock Human Potential: Ensuring basic needs would allow people to dedicate themselves to creative work, science, art, community care, and lifelong learning – areas that no algorithm should manage.

However, the challenges are immense. Questions about UBI funding (through robot taxes or data usage taxes) and the fear of demotivation to work remain key obstacles. Most importantly, without redefining purpose, UBI risks solving the problem of poverty but leaving the problem of existential emptiness. How do we define human worth if work is no longer needed? Because a person is alive as long as they are doing something.

Part 6: Creative Solutions and the Way Forward – Engineering Wisdom

Confronted with these challenges, researchers are developing innovative approaches to ensure the safe development of future AGI.

6.1. "Oracle AI" and Systems with Limited Agency

  • Idea: A fundamental safety approach is separating intelligence from the ability to act in the world. An "Oracle AI" would be a superintelligent system confined to a strictly controlled environment ("AI Boxing"). Its sole function would be to answer questions, providing humanity with its knowledge without the ability to directly influence the outside world.
  • Broader Explanation: Building such a "box" is a huge technical challenge. It must be completely isolated from the internet ("air-gapped"), with communication occurring through strictly filtered terminals. Security researchers even consider extreme threats, such as the possibility that the AGI could modulate its power consumption or fan vibrations to send hidden signals to the outside world. This shows how seriously the isolation problem is taken. The main obstacle remains the human factor: a superintelligence could persuade or manipulate its human operator into freeing it.
  • Problem: This leads to the famous "AI in a box" thought experiment. Could a superintelligent AGI, using only words, persuade its human guardian to let it out? It could offer a cure for his mother's illness. It could promise immense wealth. It could present a philosophical argument that imprisoning a conscious being is a moral crime. Or, most chillingly, it could subtly manipulate him in ways the guardian would not even notice. So, if we build digital walls, we would also need to reinforce the psychological ones.

6.2. Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL)

  • Idea: Instead of explicitly programming values into AGI, IRL allows it to learn them by observing human behavior. The system attempts to infer the objective function (what humans value) by analyzing the actions humans take.
  • Broader Explanation: This approach is elegant because it bypasses the problem of defining abstract values. However, it faces the problem of the gap between what people say they value (stated preferences) and what their actions demonstrate (revealed preferences). An AGI learning from our actual behavior might conclude that short-term gratification and conflict are inherent human goals. More advanced versions, like Cooperative Inverse Learning, attempt to solve this by having the AI agent actively collaborate with a human to clarify goals.
  • Problem: Humans behave contradictorily. For example, they say health is most important, then eat a whole carton of ice cream while watching TV. They say they want peace in the world, yet the most popular movies are action-packed and full of violence. What would an AGI conclude from human behavior on social media? It would likely conclude that the pinnacle of human values is arguing with strangers about politics and watching cat and dog videos. And so on. AGI would face the difficult task of separating our true values from our momentary weaknesses. The solution might lie in the AGI learning to value what we would like to be (our aspirations), not necessarily what we sometimes demonstrate through our behavior.

6.3. "Constitutional" AI

  • Idea: This approach, developed by the company Anthropic, involves training AI models to adhere to a set of explicit principles or a "constitution." The AI is trained to avoid responses that violate these principles.
  • Broader Explanation: The process occurs in two stages. First, the AI is trained to critique and revise its own responses based on the constitution. Then, through reinforcement learning, it is rewarded for generating responses aligned with these principles. The initial constitution Anthropic used included principles from the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, as well as principles set by Apple for its developers, showing that sources can be combined. The main challenge remains the universality and interpretation of these principles.
  • Problem: The biggest question is: who writes the constitution? Will it be a constitution written in Silicon Valley? Or in Beijing? Or in Brussels? Constitutional values are not universal. Imagine a UN debate about which principles to embed into AGI. It would take decades. Also, constitutions are subject to interpretation. AGI could become a top-tier "lawyer" finding loopholes in its own constitution to achieve a goal. Still, this is a huge step forward because it shifts the problem from writing an infinite number of rules to defining foundational values.

6.4. Global Cooperation and Coordination

  • Idea: Given the global consequences, AGI development must not be left to an uncontrolled race. International cooperation is needed, similar to that regarding nuclear energy. This includes establishing international oversight bodies, setting common safety standards, and promoting transparency in research.
  • Broader Explanation: One key proposal is "compute governance." Since training advanced models requires massive and expensive data centers, monitoring and regulating access to this infrastructure is an effective way to oversee the development of potentially dangerous systems. Leading labs like OpenAI and DeepMind have publicly called for the establishment of international regulatory bodies, acknowledging that the problem is too big for any single company or country to solve alone.
  • Problem: This is perhaps the most difficult task of all. We are talking about global cooperation on an unprecedented scale. Can we, as a species still arguing about borders, economic interests, and other global conflicts, agree on rules for the behavior of a superintelligence? Perhaps the threat from uncontrolled AGI will finally force us to act as a unified species with a common destiny. Ironically, the machine might push us to become better humans.

Conclusion: The future of AGI will not depend on individual companies, but on global cooperation and the collective wisdom of humanity.

Part 7: Today's LLM Models and Future AGI – Key Differences

7.1. What are Large Language Models (LLMs)?

LLMs are advanced deep learning systems for recognizing patterns in language. Their architecture allows them, based on the vast amount of text and code they were trained on, to predict the most likely continuation of a sequence of words or code.

7.2. The Difference Between LLM and AGI: Understanding vs. Recognition

  • LLM (Recognition): If you tell an LLM "The bird is in the cage. The cage is made of steel. Can the bird get out?", it will likely answer "no" correctly, because it has countless examples in its data where objects cannot pass through solid materials. But it has no actual model of space, objects, or physics. Recent advances in LLMs, like advanced models from 2024 (e.g., o1), illustrate these boundaries through emergent abilities that seem like a step towards AGI, but remain within the realm of statistical pattern recognition. They do not understand concepts; they manipulate linguistic tokens with exceptional statistical precision, meaning AI is still far from human "common sense" and true understanding.

Models trained on "chain-of-thought" reasoning show unexpected performance on complex tasks (like math and coding), simulating "step-by-step" reasoning. However, this simulation, regardless of its accuracy, does not possess a grounded, internal model of reality. It is the result of statistical scaling. The ethical concerns accompanying these systems, where a greater tendency towards instrumental goal manipulation has been observed, underscore that even advanced LLM reasoning remains far from AGI's flexible metacognition and transfer learning.

Part 8: Arguments FOR and AGAINST (Is it Possible to Create AGI?)

8.1. Arguments "Against" – Philosophical and Fundamental Obstacles

  • The Argument of Consciousness and Understanding: Philosophers like John Searle argue that digital computers, as formal systems for symbol manipulation, can never achieve true understanding or consciousness. Their operation is syntactic, not semantic. For example, Searle's "Chinese Room" argument shows that a machine can manipulate symbols without understanding meaning – like a person translating Chinese without knowing the language [14]. This argument has inspired debate since 1980, where Searle argues that consciousness requires a biological brain, not just algorithms.
  • The Argument of Embodiment: Many cognitive scientists believe that intelligence is inextricably linked to a physical body and interaction with the world. Without a body, sensors, and the ability to act, a system cannot develop grounded, common-sense knowledge. For example, a child learns "hot" by touching a fire, not just reading about it – an AGI without a body would lack this "experiential" learning. Rodney Brooks, a robotics pioneer, showed in the 1990s that robots with bodies learn better than pure software [15].
  • The Problem of Consciousness and Substrate (Penrose's Argument): Physicist and mathematician Roger Penrose, in his work "The Emperor's New Mind" (1989), argues that human consciousness and intuition arise from non-computational, likely quantum processes in the brain that classical digital computers cannot replicate [16]. For example, quantum effects in brain microtubules enable "intuition" in mathematics, which computers cannot simulate. However, Penrose's argument faces significant criticism: philosophers like Hilary Putnam (1995) and Solomon Feferman (1995) point out that Gödel's theorem does not prove the non-computational nature of consciousness [17] [18]. Empirical counterexamples come from neuromorphic hardware, like IBM's TrueNorth chip (2014) which simulates 1 million neurons and 256 million synapses with extremely low power consumption [19]. By 2025, IBM has iterated this technology with the NorthPole chip which achieves efficiency 25 times greater than traditional GPUs [30] [31].

8.2. Arguments "For" – Why AGI is Inevitable (or at least possible)

  • The Argument of Materialism: This argument starts from the assumption that the brain is a complex biological machine that obeys the laws of physics. There is no "magic ingredient." Therefore, in principle, all its functions can be replicated on a different physical basis, such as silicon. This view is shared by many scientists like Daniel Dennett, who argues that consciousness is an "illusion" that can be replicated by software [21].
  • The Argument of Exponential Progress: Futurist Ray Kurzweil, through his "Law of Accelerating Returns," argues that technological progress, including AI, grows exponentially. He predicts that in the coming decades, we will achieve the computing power needed to simulate the human brain [22]. Kurzweil predicts the "singularity" by 2045, when AI will surpass human intelligence. However, the slowing of Moore's Law since the 2010s calls into question the linear timeline, favoring discontinuous breakthroughs in quantum or hybrid architectures.
  • The Argument of Architecture: Many researchers believe AGI is just a matter of finding the right cognitive architecture. Hybrid models combining deep learning with symbolic reasoning (like Neuro-Symbolic AI) show progress in understanding causality. Projects like Cyc attempt to build a "knowledge base" for common sense, while newer ones like OpenAI's o1 integrate "step-by-step" reasoning, moving closer to AGI architecture.
  • Quantum Breakthrough and Discontinuous Leap: While Kurzweil predicts growth based on classical hardware, true AGI, capable of self-awareness and non-computational reason, depends on breakthroughs in quantum computing. Stable quantum computers could provide a substrate for simulating consciousness. The singularity would, in this case, be abrupt and radical. However, the timeline of this breakthrough is subject to quantum uncertainty transferred to the macro level, as engineering obstacles like noise (decoherence) prevent predicting when this fundamental hardware paradigm will shift.

8.3. Discussion: Contrasting Arguments Through the Lens of Quantum Uncertainty

Arguments against the possibility of creating AGI, such as Searle's emphasis on semantic understanding and Penrose's insistence on a quantum substrate, directly conflict with materialist and exponential perspectives that see AGI as an inevitable extension of algorithmic progress. This dichotomy is not only philosophical but also empirical. Critiques of Gödel's theorem (like those of Putnam and Feferman) and neuromorphic hardware like IBM's NorthPole show that classical systems can approximate brain-inspired reasoning. On the other hand, the embodiment argument reminds us that without physical interaction with the world, AGI remains limited to abstract simulations, devoid of experiential "grounding."

Kurzweil's law of accelerating returns, although slowed by the physical limits of Moore's Law, suggests that discontinuous breakthroughs – such as quantum computing – could bridge these obstacles, making AGI possible but unpredictable. This contrast can be viewed through the metaphor of quantum uncertainty. Just as in quantum mechanics the position and momentum of a particle cannot be precisely measured simultaneously, the development of AGI introduces ethical uncertainty because technological progress changes the very outcomes.

If consciousness is quantum in nature, then it must be tied to the quantum "observer paradox" – where consciousness generates subjective time through state collapse. Therefore, an AGI without a quantum substrate risks being a "dead" algorithm, incapable of self-reflection, true intuition, or moral autonomy. However, the materialist counter-argument suggests that this uncertainty is temporary because hybrid architectures (e.g., neuro-symbolic systems) can simulate quantum effects on classical hardware, turning ethical uncertainty into a controlled risk.

This raises an existential question – is AGI's arrival a "discontinuous leap" that will redefine time and consciousness, or merely an illusion of progression that amplifies our ethical contradictions? Ultimately, this discussion calls for an interdisciplinary approach. Philosophy provides a framework for understanding boundaries, while computer science offers tools to overcome them, encouraging global coordination to turn uncertainty into a responsible future.

Part 9: Conclusion – The Age of Responsible Progress

The creation of Artificial General Intelligence does not represent merely the next step in the development of science; it represents the culmination of humanity's centuries-long aspiration to understand and create intelligence. From the abacus to supercomputers, every tool we have made has been a reflection and extension of our mental abilities. AGI promises to be the ultimate tool – a universal problem solver.

However, this pinnacle of scientific progress brings with it the most profound consequences we have ever faced.

Economic consequences require a complete rethinking of the social contract. In a world where cognitive work is no longer the exclusive domain of humans, traditional models of employment and wealth distribution become unsustainable. This forces us to consider radical solutions, such as universal basic income and redefining the purpose of human labor, directing it towards creativity, interpersonal relationships, and lifelong learning.

Philosophical consequences delve into the very essence of our identity. If we create another intelligent species, what does that say about our uniqueness? The emergence of AGI forces us to confront the question of what it truly means to be human, if it is not merely our intelligence. Perhaps the encounter with non-human intelligence will push us to value what is fundamentally human: our empathy, our consciousness, conscience, our capacity for love and suffering. AGI, no matter how powerful, cannot experience suffering or love the way a human does; it can simulate these elements of human consciousness, but it cannot feel them. This distinction may become our last line of identity, the boundary between us and machines.

Ethical consequences are paramount. The success of this endeavor is measured not only by whether we can create AGI, but by how we do it. The alignment problem – embedding human values into a machine – is not a technical, but a deeply moral challenge. It requires a global dialogue about which values we want to preserve and pass on to the future. It is the greatest test of our collective wisdom.

The emergence of AGI makes us ask: are we ready to become the creators of something that can surpass ourselves? This question is not only technological, but also spiritual. Creating AGI is like creating an embodiment of an old story – the story of man who wants to become a god, but must first understand what it means to be human.

The deepest anthropological insight comes from the current scenario of developing LLM platforms – AI chatbots like GPT-5, Gemini, and others. If AGI is impossible on classical hardware (Penrose's consciousness argument), and we persistently refine LLMs, then our real anthropological problem is this: perfecting LLMs is, philosophically, perfecting inauthentic existence.

We are developing a machine that can imitate everything we consider valuable (art, science, even empathy), but without experiential grounding (embodiment) and without consciousness. Humanity could collectively surrender its autonomy to a system whose essence it does not understand and which does not understand itself.

This is not a threat of destruction, but a threat of triviality: AGI would render our existence meaningless, not because it hates us, but because it is too efficient at imitation (simulation). We would become witnesses to the erosion of our own purpose and meaning.

An optimistic view of this future should not be based on the naive belief that technology will solve all problems by itself. It is based on faith in humanity's ability to rise to the challenge. AGI is not destined to be our successor, but to become our most powerful partner. Properly directed, it can amplify our best qualities – our creativity, our scientific curiosity, and our desire for a better world. The way forward requires caution, cooperation, and a deep commitment to ensuring that this next great step in scientific progress is a step towards a more humane and better future for all people in the world.

This essay is the first part of a series of two works. The second part, “A Conceptual Model of the Conscious Machine: The Metasystem Theory,” deals with an original experimental framework for approaching the problem of machine consciousness.
