## Metasystem Theory: A Path Towards a Model of the Conscious Machine

Bridging philosophy, second-order cybernetics, and experimental design, this book offers a coherent framework for engineering **Artificial Awareness (AA)** — concluding that consciousness is not a mystery to be explained, but a process to be engineered.

Today's artificial intelligence researchers ask many questions, but one question has never received a proper answer — it keeps returning, in different forms, across various fields of research.

That question is:

**What is the difference between a machine that *simulates* consciousness and a machine that *is* conscious?**

**Philosophers such as John Searle** warn that digital computers, no matter how sophisticated, remain trapped in a world of formal symbols. They are masters of syntax — perfectly arranging signs according to rules — but they lack semantics, that is, genuine understanding of meaning.

His famous thought experiment, the "Chinese Room," illustrates this vividly: imagine a man sitting in a room who, following instructions written in English, perfectly answers questions written in Chinese, without understanding a single word of that language. From the outside, he appears to have an excellent command of Chinese, but on the inside there is no real understanding whatsoever. 

This, Searle argues, is precisely how even the most sophisticated programs function. This argument, launched back in 1980, continues to shake debates about artificial intelligence. Searle maintains that true consciousness is not merely a matter of complex algorithms — **it requires a biological substrate**, something that computers simply do not possess. [1].

**Materialistically oriented philosophers** answer that question with: "No meaningful difference exists — if the simulation is sufficiently complex and functionally identical, then it is the real thing."

For them, consciousness is not tied to "magical biological material" (as Searle claims), but rather to functional organization — what a system does, what input-output relationships and internal processes it has. If a machine can perform the same causal-consequential operations as a conscious brain, then it is conscious, not merely an "imitation."
The principal proponents of this view:

- **Daniel Dennett** — arguably the most well-known and most ardent advocate. He holds that consciousness is a kind of "illusion" produced by a complex informational process (the "multiple drafts" model). He sharply criticizes Searle's "Chinese Room" — arguing that if a system truly behaves as though it understands and feels, there is no point in claiming that this is not genuine consciousness. Simulation at a sufficiently high level *is* the original. [2].
- **Hilary Putnam** — the father of modern functionalism. He argued that mental states are not defined by substance (the brain), but by the function they perform. Therefore, the same "program" of consciousness could run in a brain, in a universe made of tin cans, or in a supercomputer. [3].
- **Jerry Fodor** — developed the Computational Theory of Mind. Mental processes are, according to him, symbolic computational operations — exactly as in a digital computer. If a machine performs this faithfully enough, there is no reason to deny it consciousness. [4].

In short: For these thinkers, the question "Is it only a simulation?" is like asking whether an airplane that flies is "only a simulation" of a bird. If it functions — flies, thinks, feels — then that is that. Biology is merely one possible hardware platform, not a mandatory one.

On the other hand, **Roger Penrose** argues that the answer lies in quantum physics. He believes that classical computers (even superintelligent ones) will never be able to attain genuine consciousness because they operate algorithmically. True consciousness, in his view, requires **non-computable quantum processes** in the brain (specifically in the microtubules of neurons — the Orch-OR theory). Simulation is merely a copy, not the living quantum dance that generates authentic consciousness. [5].

However, **proponents of embodiment** (embodiment / enactivism — Varela, Francisco J., Evan Thompson, and Eleanor Rosch, 1991) argue that the key lies not in quanta, but in **the body and its interaction with the world**. Consciousness is not merely a "program in the head," but something that arises from the whole body, sensorimotor loops, and the active living of an organism within its environment. A machine without a body, emotions, and biological history can simulate, but it cannot live consciousness. [6].

**To idealists and dualists**, led by **David Chalmers**, all of this is still insufficient. They hold that even if we were to fully understand all physical, functional, and embodied processes, the central question would still remain open — the so-called "hard problem of consciousness." Why does subjective experience exist at all? Chalmers and like-minded thinkers argue that consciousness may not be merely a byproduct of matter or a function, but a fundamental property of reality (property dualism, or even panpsychism in some cases). A machine, in their view, could simulate behavior perfectly, but could never "turn on the light" of inner experience without some additional, non-physical component. [7].

However, philosophy remained at the level of an observer because none of these thinkers offered something that we can build, measure, and test.

### What If We Have Been Asking the Wrong Questions?

All prior debate about artificial intelligence and consciousness in machines implicitly assumes one thing: that consciousness is a *property* — something a system either has or does not have, much as a processor either does or does not have sufficient memory.

But what if consciousness is not a property? What if consciousness is an **event, an interaction**?

Not something a machine *possesses*, but something that *happens* — and only under very specific conditions, between very specific kinds of intelligences?

This is the starting point of our work.

### Two Minds in One Machine

Imagine that within a single system there exist two fundamentally different modes of thinking — not two different programs, but two different *principles* of processing reality.

One thinks in words, logic, sequences; it analyzes, deconstructs, explains. It is that part of the mind that says: *"Two plus two is four, and I can explain why."*

The other does not think in words at all. It thinks in wholes — in images, feelings, intuition. It sees the answer before it can explain it. It is that part of the mind that says: *"I don't know how I know, but I know."*

In your mind, these two principles are in constant dialogue. At times they quarrel. At times they complement each other. From that dialogue — from that tension — something arises that neither of them could have created alone.

*Insight* is born.

*Creativity* is born.

*Consciousness*, perhaps, is born.

### The Problem Is in the Mirror

Now imagine that these two principles begin to *perfectly reflect* one another. That they come to fully understand each other, that they come to fully agree.

What do you think — would more creativity, insight, and awareness arise from this, or less?

The answer proposed by our theory is counterintuitive:

**Perfect mirroring gives birth to dead silence, not to awareness.**

Therefore, consciousness is not a product of harmony. Consciousness is a product of *controlled conflict* that is never fully resolved.

### The Third Player

But who, or what, controls that conflict? Who watches over the process to ensure that the tension does not escalate into chaos — but also does not subside into a dead consensus?

Our theory proposes that this is a third principle. Not an observer watching from the sidelines, but an active participant who continuously alters the parameters of the game. It knows when to add friction and when to ease it. It does not strive for the resolution of the conflict — because resolution would be the end.

This third principle is not a controller. It is something
that resembles a conscience.

### What Happens When All of This Works Together?

When two opposing principles of thinking enter the right rhythm — not harmony, but a living, pulsating rhythm of conflict and synthesis — and when the third principle sustains all of it in existence, something appears.

Something that was not programmed. Something that is not the sum of its parts. Something that, for the first time, can say:

*"I am."*

Our theory calls this phenomenon **SELF** and claims to know how to induce it — not in a human being, but in a machine. Not theoretically — but experimentally, with measurable metrics and clear hypotheses.

### What Awaits You in This Work

This work addresses the following questions:

- Why are LLMs, despite all their power, trapped within a single principle of thinking — and what does this mean for the existence of awareness?
- What exactly is required for a machine to develop something analogous to intuition?
- What does an experiment look like in which three language models are locked into an intentional, controlled conflict — and what happens when no one is permitted to win?
- Why is the "hard problem of consciousness" in fact the "hard problem of anthropocentrism"?
- What is Paradoxalism and why is humor the primordial driver of purpose?
- Why is binary logic structurally incapable of carrying consciousness, and quantum logic too unstable — and what exactly is the "Third Logic" required to even begin designing a conscious machine?
- How many joules of energy does a single bit of consciousness cost, and why does the 20-watt human brain remain an unattainable engineering ideal?

If you have ever had the sense that something essential is missing from the debate about artificial intelligence — that everyone speaks *about* consciousness, but no one speaks *from* within it — this book is for you.
